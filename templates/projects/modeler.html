The gap

Computational thinking — breaking a problem down, finding the pattern in it, working out the steps, abstracting what matters — is a general problem-solving skill that happens to be taught inside computer science. Classrooms that are not computer science rarely get to it, especially in younger grades where the teacher may not have the background themselves.

Modeler closes that gap. It gives students a structure for modelling a topic that would not normally be modelled — the relationships between characters in a novel, say — in three steps: diagram the relationships with shapes, arrows and labels; attach measurements or rates of change to them; then watch the system turn that description into a running simulation.

My role

I defined the user flow and designed the interface. To understand what to carry across, I talked with computer science professors at Michigan Tech about which parts of learning to program actually transfer to other subjects, and worked from the principal investigator's direction throughout.

The visual design had to feel like educational software without feeling dated — the PI wanted a retro register, particularly early on. The piece I pushed hardest for was a place to reflect: somewhere students take notes on what they are doing and why. Watching a simulation run is not the same as understanding it.

Explaining it to people outside the project

I animated the early interface prototype walking through a basic photosynthesis model. It gave the developers a clear picture of how the pages relate and what each component does.

An animation rather than a static spec was a deliberate choice: the PI needed something he could show to people with no exposure to the design or development process, like professors in other departments and high school teachers.
